\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first compare reachability decisions and their supporting traces, then use the trace diagnostics to examine why their reliability differs. Each model is evaluated on the same 46,656 questions, including non-responses. Detailed curves and diagnostic denominators appear in Appendix~\ref{app:result-details}.

\subsection{\researchq{1}: Reachability Accuracy and Structural Factors}

\subsubsection{Correct Decisions Do Not Always Have Valid Evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:overall} shows that Qwen obtains the highest overall scores: 87.98\% final-answer correctness and 67.43\% path correctness, compared with 84.21\% and 55.90\% for Gemma. The advantage is therefore larger for the supporting trace than for the Boolean decision. In this experiment, every correct path accompanies a correct answer. The converse does not hold: 28.31\% of Gemma responses and 20.55\% of Qwen responses have a correct answer but an incorrect path.

\input{tables/overall_performance}

The family split locates much of this difference. Without guards, Qwen reaches 99.19\% answer correctness and 96.33\% path correctness; Gemma reaches 96.05\% and 82.23\%. In condition-aware programs, the scores fall to 84.24\% and 57.80\% for Qwen, and 80.26\% and 47.13\% for Gemma. Non-responses account for 1.10\% and 2.73\% of condition-aware questions, far less than the answer--path gaps. The main difficulty therefore concerns the executable evidence returned by the models, beyond recovering a parsable answer.

\subsubsection{Conditions and Parameters Act Together}

Table~\ref{tab:factors} reports contrasts within the condition-aware group. Increasing the number of guards from one to five reduces path correctness by 21.94 points for Gemma and 19.17 points for Qwen. However, the largest parameter contrast occurs when the first parameter is introduced. Gemma's path score falls from 66.62\% to 39.17\%, and Qwen's from 90.53\% to 43.52\%. A second parameter produces a smaller further decrease (Table~\ref{tab:factors}).

\input{tables/factor_effects}

The joint configuration is more informative than either marginal curve. With no guards, changing the parameter count from zero to two reduces path correctness by only 2.26 points for Gemma and 0.44 for Qwen. With five guards, the corresponding reductions are 33.82 and 55.66 points. Parameters are thus particularly difficult when their values determine which calls can execute. This interaction motivates the value-origin analysis in \researchq{2}; it does not, by itself, isolate value propagation from the additional text introduced by these factors.

\subsubsection{Distance and Class Size Affect Different Parts of the Task}

For positive condition-aware questions, increasing nominal distance from 1 to 25 reduces path correctness from 95.68\% to 56.58\% for Gemma and from 99.90\% to 70.52\% for Qwen. For negative questions, it falls from 54.01\% to 11.63\% and from 55.56\% to 22.94\%. The executable graph helps explain this asymmetry. At magnitude 25, a positive query has an average shortest executable route of 5.90 methods, because additional calls can bypass parts of the generated chain. The negative region contains 26 methods and must be covered completely. Positive and negative queries at the same nominal distance therefore impose different evidence requirements.

\vspace{0.2cm}

Class size also separates decisions from evidence. Between 100 and 500 methods, Gemma's condition-aware path score decreases from 55.70\% to 37.23\%, and Qwen's from 60.11\% to 54.99\%. Qwen's answer score nevertheless increases from 82.46\% to 85.67\%. Section~\ref{subsec:context-mechanism} examines the opposing polarity-specific changes behind this average. Comments, local variables, and loops have smaller marginal associations with path correctness in this grid: none exceeds five points for either model. This observation applies to their generated forms; it does not imply that these constructs are generally easy in real programs.

\paragraph{Answer to \researchq{1}.}
Qwen performs best overall, but both models are substantially less reliable at constructing executable evidence than at producing the final decision. The strongest degradations involve conditions, parameters used with conditions, and the exhaustive exploration required by negative questions.

\subsection{\researchq{2}: What the Traces Explain}

\subsubsection{Visible Calls Can Be Mistaken for Executable Calls}

For condition-aware questions, Gemma's path correctness is 69.27\% on positive queries but 24.99\% on negative queries; Qwen's is 81.57\% and 34.04\%. The distinction is not simply whether a method name can be recalled. On negative questions, a returned trace includes at least one existing method outside the executable region in 51.38\% of Gemma responses and 48.50\% of Qwen responses. The mean omitted fraction of the true region is also large: 39.37\% and 38.01\%. A trace can therefore both enter a disabled branch and leave part of the executable region unexplored. These diagnostics overlap and should not be added as error categories.


Following a call behind a false guard can invalidate the trace without changing the Boolean answer. Its consequence depends on where that call leads. Among unambiguously attributed guard errors, the share whose disabled callees can reach the target rises from 9.24\% to 36.41\% for Gemma, and from 11.19\% to 36.63\% for Qwen, as \texttt{nb\_if} increases from one to five. Over the same range, Qwen's advantage on positive over negative answers grows from 8.25 to 21.47 points; Gemma's grows from 0.15 to 7.85 points. Both polarities become less accurate, but negative decisions deteriorate more sharply. This pattern is consistent with entering disabled branches that expose the target and encourage a false positive.

\vspace{0.2cm}

The association can be checked directly in eligible negative responses. When the trace contains an attributed guard error leading toward the target, the answer is a false positive in 89.73\% of Gemma cases and 92.57\% of Qwen cases. Without such an attributed error, the corresponding rates are 1.36\% and 0.46\%. Ambiguous attributions are excluded from both comparisons, so these rates do not characterize every negative response. They describe an association within generated outputs: the answer and the trace may reflect the same mistake, and the observation does not establish which caused the other.

\subsubsection{Value Propagation Is More Informative Than Parameter Count Alone}
\label{subsec:value-origin}

Without parameters, only 0.25\% of Gemma's eligible guard exposures and 0.18\% of Qwen's are attributed to false-guard errors. With one parameter, these ratios increase to 7.42\% and 9.01\%; with two, they reach 10.40\% and 11.11\%. Meanwhile, the mean omitted fraction on negative questions increases from 12.76\% to 51.04\% for Gemma, and from 3.62\% to 53.75\% for Qwen, between zero and one parameter. The parameter effect thus involves both following non-executable calls and failing to cover executable ones.


The distance to the declaration supplying a guard's value distinguishes the exposed guards with and without attributed errors (Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic-definitions}). With one parameter, Gemma's attributed-error guards have an average origin distance of 5.41 method boundaries, compared with 3.59 for exposed guards without an attributed error. Qwen shows the same separation, 5.15 versus 3.58. With two parameters, the pairs become 6.22 versus 4.47 and 5.90 versus 4.47. On their common unambiguous attribution population, mean exposure distance is almost identical between models: 3.72 with one parameter and approximately 4.64 with two. Their errors are therefore concentrated on more distant value origins than their other exposed guards, under comparable measured exposure difficulty.

\vspace{0.2cm}

These averages weight guard occurrences and exclude literal conditions, unresolved origins, and ambiguous attributions. Guards without an attributed error are not necessarily evaluated correctly. The separation therefore locates errors around longer value dependencies; it does not estimate a failure probability per additional boundary. The reference-exploration comparison and its different weighting are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic-definitions}.

This also qualifies Qwen's overall advantage. Its condition-aware path lead is 23.91 points without parameters, but only 4.35 with one parameter and 3.75 with two. Its attributed guard-error rate per exposure is higher than Gemma's despite its higher path accuracy. Gemma also has the higher negative answer score in the condition-aware group: 78.10\% versus 75.85\%. Qwen's overall lead therefore does not establish uniformly better guard evaluation or decision accuracy across polarities. Path correctness combines several requirements and depends on which calls the model exposes.

\subsubsection{Larger Classes Change the Consequences of Errors}
\label{subsec:context-mechanism}

Qwen's rising mean answer score with class size conceals opposite movements. Between 100 and 500 methods, positive answer correctness decreases from 97.67\% to 86.68\%, while negative correctness increases from 67.25\% to 84.67\%. Gemma shows an even stronger shift: positive correctness falls from 96.04\% to 66.03\%, whereas negative correctness rises from 69.24\% to 87.64\%. In both models, positive and negative path correctness decline over the same range. A higher negative answer score therefore does not indicate more complete exploration.


The guard diagnostics connect this shift to the location of the target (Appendix~\ref{app:result-details}). The attributed error rate per exposure changes only modestly, from 4.06\% to 4.53\% for Gemma and from 5.04\% to 5.63\% for Qwen. In contrast, the target-directed share of attributed guard errors decreases from 32.36\% to 8.90\% and from 35.27\% to 13.62\%. The models do not show better local guard handling as the class grows; their attributed errors instead lead toward the target less often. A plausible explanation is that a disabled branch in a larger generated graph is less likely to intersect the queried target. Increasing omission also gives an incomplete exploration more opportunities to miss a genuinely reachable target. These two changes are compatible with fewer false positives and more false negatives, even as trace quality worsens. They explain why balanced answer accuracy alone is insufficient to assess context robustness.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\paragraph{Answer to \researchq{2}.}
The traces connect the performance losses to errors about which calls can execute, omissions during exhaustive exploration, and longer value dependencies. Whether an erroneous branch leads to the target helps explain both the positive-answer advantage under guards and its reduction in larger classes. The evidence supports these mechanisms as hypotheses grounded in the returned traces, rather than causal claims about internal reasoning.

\subsection{\researchq{3}: Relation to Existing Benchmarks}

Qwen ranks above Gemma in the published LiveCodeBench~v6, SWE-bench, and Terminal-Bench~2.0 comparisons for the named checkpoints~\cite{qwen2026qwen36,google2026gemma4}, agreeing with its overall lead on \benchmark. Appendix~\ref{app:external-comparison} records the vendor-reported scores and protocols. These evaluations use different tasks, budgets, and scaffolds, including Qwen's internal agent scaffold for SWE-bench; we did not reproduce them. Their score gaps cannot be transferred to reachability. The agreement in ordering also conceals the shared difficulty with passed values observed here.

\paragraph{Answer to \researchq{3}.}
The published comparisons show the same overall ordering as \benchmark. This is descriptive concordance for two checkpoints under different protocols, not evidence of a general relationship between benchmark scores.
